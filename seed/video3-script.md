### Buttons on thick fabric: denim, coats, and shank buttons

Last time we sewed a button that never falls off again, using the toothpick trick to leave slack and wrapping a thread shank under the button. On thin fabric that is the whole story. On thick fabric — jeans, a wool overcoat — the same button fails for a different reason: the fabric itself needs room, and the load needs to be spread. This video covers three cases: denim, thick wool coats, and shank buttons with the metal loop on the back. At the end, one line on which thread to buy and whether to wax it.

#### 1. Denim: the jeans button that keeps going

Jeans buttons fail because denim is stiff and the button gets levered sideways every time you sit down. Three fixes.

First, if it is a sewn button, sew it with the thread doubled, through all layers of the waistband, not just the top layer. The waistband is your anchor; the top layer alone will tear.

Second, make the thread shank longer than you think — on denim, wrap the thread under the button six to eight times, not three. The shank is what lets the button lean without pulling the stitches through.

Third, put a small flat backing button on the inside of the waistband and sew through both buttons at once. The backing button spreads the load across the fabric so the stitches cannot pull through, no matter how often the button is stressed.

If your jeans have the hammer-on metal tack button and the whole thing pulled out, do not try to re-hammer it into the torn hole. Move a replacement tack button half a centimetre to the side, into fresh denim, and squeeze it closed with pliers over a folded cloth.

#### 2. Thick wool coat buttons

A coat button carries more weight than any other button you own, and thick wool compresses. If you sew the button down tight, the buttonhole side has nowhere to sit, and the button pops within a week.

The rule: the thread shank must be as long as the fabric is thick, plus two millimetres. Lay a matchstick or a bamboo skewer on top of the button while you sew to force that slack, then lift it out and wrap the shank firmly from top to bottom.

Always use a backing button inside the coat facing — a plain flat button about the size of your fingernail. Every stitch goes through the coat button, the wool, and the backing button in one pass. Six passes of doubled thread is enough; more just bulks the shank.

Sew coat buttons with waxed doubled thread. Thick wool eats untreated thread; the wax lets it pull through cleanly and tightens the wrap.

#### 3. Shank buttons: the metal loop on the back

A shank button already has its stand-off built in — the metal or plastic loop is the shank. So you do not use the toothpick trick here.

Orient the loop so it lies parallel to the buttonhole. Anchor your doubled thread in the fabric, then take small stitches through the loop and the fabric, alternating direction, eight to ten passes.

On a thick coat, a shank button still gets a backing button on the inside, same as before — the metal loop does not spread load, it concentrates it, so the backing button matters more here, not less.

If the shank loop has worn through the old thread, check the loop edge with your fingernail before re-sewing: a burr on the metal will saw through the new thread too. File it smooth or replace the button.

#### 4. Thread: what to buy, and the wax question

One line, as promised: buy Gütermann all-purpose polyester — the everyday spool — and for jeans and coats step up to Gütermann Extra Strong or any topstitch-weight polyester. And yes, wax it for hand sewing: pull the thread twice across a block of beeswax and press it once with a warm iron between paper so the wax sets into the fibre. Waxed thread glides through thick fabric and grips the wrap you make under the button.

That covers denim, coats, and shank buttons. The buttons you sew this way outlive the garment.
